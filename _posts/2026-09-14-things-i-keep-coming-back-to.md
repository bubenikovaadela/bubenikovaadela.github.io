*On understanding, attention, chance, and the danger of making everything useful*

I keep far too many notes from books, papers, essays and conversations. They are rarely proper summaries, I usually copy one sentence that made me stop, add no context, and trust that a future version of me will know why it mattered, haha.

I went back through some of them recently and realised that the same questions keep reappearing. Not in a neat way, and definitely not in a way that adds up to a philosophy. More like recurring irritations.


## Understanding is not recognition

Nabeel Qureshi’s *How to Understand Things* keeps returning to a distinction I find increasingly important: the difference between being able to follow an explanation and being able to reconstruct it for oneself.[1] Some people seem almost constitutionally unwilling to accept the first as evidence of the second. They change the representation, redraw the problem, ask what each step depends on, and continue until the explanation can survive without the language in which they first encountered it.

What interests me is how rarely we notice when we have stopped short of that point.

Familiarity has the texture of understanding. Once the vocabulary becomes comfortable, the diagram recognisable, and the sequence of an argument familiar, the mind quietly upgrades recognition into knowledge. Rozenblit and Keil gave this tendency a useful name: the *illusion of explanatory depth*.[2] A zipper, a bicycle, a flush toilet all appear perfectly intelligible until one is asked to describe, step by step, what actually happens. Scientific ideas are no different. We can become fluent in the vocabulary of a field without ever quite acquiring its internal grammar.

There is something almost embarrassing about how quickly an idea changes once the source is taken away. A mechanism that seemed obvious becomes difficult to redraw. A sentence depends on a term that had been doing more conceptual work than we realised. A model begins to reveal not only what it explains, but what it has quietly decided not to explain.

Writing is useful for exactly this reason. Thoughts are hospitable places for ambiguity; sentences are less forgiving. On the page, the missing transition acquires a shape.

This is what I like in Feynman’s idea of scientific integrity. His warning that “the first principle is that you must not fool yourself” is almost banal until one notices how much intelligence can be used in the service of self-deception.[3] Intelligence is very good at constructing arguments. It is less naturally inclined to dismantle the arguments it wants to believe.

And yet understanding has its own pathology. One can insist on completeness so rigidly that nothing is ever finished. Medicine, science and ordinary life all require us to move forward while uncertainty remains. There is no clean border between intellectual patience and paralysis, just as there is no clean border between necessary simplification and premature certainty.

Perhaps that is why the phrase *I understand this well enough to continue* can be either a sign of maturity or a form of escape.

## Choosing the question

Richard Hamming used to ask scientists which problems in their fields were genuinely important, and then ask why they were not working on them.[4] The second question is more interesting because it exposes a quieter form of intellectual avoidance.

There is almost always another respectable thing to do. Another analysis can be run, another cohort assembled, another variable added, another technically elegant extension made to something already known. None of this is trivial. Most serious science advances incrementally. But competence can become self-propelling. The better we become at a method, the more likely we are to keep finding reasons to use it.

Eventually, the method begins choosing the question.

This may be one of the less visible dangers of productivity. It can conceal drift. A project can be beautifully designed and almost entirely beside the point. A field can become increasingly sophisticated at measuring what it has already decided to value. A person can become very good at answering questions without noticing that the questions have become smaller.

I keep returning to a version of Hamming’s question that is less heroic and probably more useful: if I became exceptionally good at doing exactly what I am doing now, where would it lead?

The answer is not always flattering.

Not every important problem is tractable, and not every tractable problem is important. Somewhere between the two lies judgement, which is harder to teach than method because it depends on scale, timing, context and a certain willingness to leave things unresolved.

Some questions deserve to remain questions for a long time. They sit in the background before they become projects, gathering methods, people, observations and accidents around them. New information becomes meaningful because there is already something in the mind prepared to receive it.

Discovery is often described as encountering something new. I suspect it is just as often the ability to recognise significance in something that many others have already seen.

## Chance and the rooms we enter

Success stories become cleaner in retrospect. We remove the accidental introductions, the timing, the institution that made one path visible and another nearly impossible, the person who happened to take an interest, the meeting we almost did not attend. What remains is a coherent narrative of talent, discipline and intention.

Those things matter. They are simply not the whole causal story.

Merton’s work on cumulative advantage offers a less flattering but more realistic account.[5] Opportunities do not arrive independently. One advantage alters the probability of the next, which changes the probability of the one after that. A small difference in access or recognition can compound. The first invitation leads to the second room; the first mentor changes the next introduction; the first piece of credibility makes later credibility easier to acquire.

This does not make effort irrelevant. It makes causality less tidy.

A person can have worked extraordinarily hard and still owe a great deal to circumstances they did not create. There is no contradiction in that, only a discomfort with explanations that do not distribute outcomes according to merit.

Preparation matters because it changes what chance can become. Two people may encounter the same paper, the same person, the same idea, and recognise completely different possibilities in it. What we call luck is partly an event and partly a relation between an event and a mind prepared to notice it.

This is why intellectual life is shaped by rooms as much as by books. Certain environments make some kinds of thought feel ordinary and others almost impossible. They determine whether uncertainty can be admitted without embarrassment, whether basic questions are socially expensive, whether unfinished ideas are allowed to exist in public.

The best mentors often alter this atmosphere rather than giving explicit instruction. They make seriousness feel expansive rather than punitive. They take a question seriously enough that one begins to take one’s own thinking more seriously.

And there is a reciprocal problem hidden inside that. What kind of intellectual climate do we create for other people? Do conversations leave others more willing to think aloud, or more careful not to be wrong? Does knowledge open a subject or close it? Intellectual culture is not decorative. It changes what can be thought.

## Attention and permeability

Attention seems, at first, to be a simpler problem. Sophie Leroy’s work on *attention residue* describes the cognitive cost of switching between unfinished tasks: part of the mind remains attached to what came before, even after the calendar has moved on.[6] It explains a particular kind of day I know very well — one in which almost everything was defensible and almost nothing difficult moved forward.

The obvious response is to protect time. Reduce switching. Close the door.

But Hamming complicates this too. At Bell Labs, he observed that people whose doors were closed often completed more immediate work, while those whose doors were open were interrupted more but also knew more about what others were doing and where interesting problems were emerging.[4]

The image is almost too neat: the closed door protects depth, the open door admits possibility.

A mind that is constantly interrupted may never descend far enough into a problem to discover anything difficult. A mind that is perfectly protected can become magnificently concentrated on a question that stopped mattering months ago.

The tension is not really between productivity and distraction. It is between concentration and permeability. Some thoughts require insulation; others require exposure. The difficulty is that both can masquerade as virtue for too long. Focus can become blindness without announcing the transition. Openness can become noise while still calling itself curiosity.

## Against making everything useful

The tendency I find hardest to resist is the desire to make everything useful.

Exercise improves cognition. Sleep consolidates memory. Reading fiction develops empathy. Friendship builds resilience. Walking restores attention. Hobbies prevent burnout. Rest improves performance. There is almost nothing left that cannot be defended as an intervention for becoming better at something else.

Most of those claims may be true. The cumulative effect is still slightly bleak.

Josef Pieper wrote about the danger of a world organised around “total work”, in which leisure is not abolished but absorbed into the logic of usefulness.[7] The argument feels uncannily contemporary. Productivity does not need to eliminate leisure if it can simply persuade leisure to justify itself.

Rest is still permitted, but preferably because it improves tomorrow. Reading is valuable, but ideally because it expands perspective. Exercise is good, but better if it sharpens concentration. Friendship survives, provided we can also call it community. Even solitude can be repackaged as recovery.

The language changes, but the demand remains the same: produce a return.

There is something impoverishing about this, because usefulness is not neutral. It establishes a hierarchy in which one part of life exists to serve another. Enough of these hierarchies, and eventually very little is allowed to remain an end in itself.

I want books that never become references, conversations that produce no opportunity, walks that fail to improve cognition, interests pursued badly enough that they never become another field in which to perform. Not because purposelessness is morally superior, but because some things become smaller when forced to account for their value.

The problem with an instrumental life is not that it lacks pleasure. It can contain plenty of pleasure, rest, beauty and friendship. The problem is subtler: all of them begin to require a defence.

Ambition makes this especially difficult because ambition can genuinely enlarge a life. It can organise attention, sustain years of effort, make difficult things possible and create forms of competence that would otherwise never exist. There is nothing inherently impoverishing about wanting very much from one’s work.

The danger begins when ambition becomes a solvent, gradually dissolving whatever cannot justify itself in its language.

A life composed entirely of means eventually loses sight of its ends. And perhaps the most unsettling part is that this rarely feels like loss while it is happening. It feels like seriousness, discipline, momentum, commitment. The vocabulary of achievement is unusually good at disguising what it consumes.

Maybe this is why I have become more suspicious of optimisation than of laziness. Laziness usually knows what it is. Optimisation can make surrender look like progress.

## The useful part is the friction

What keeps bringing me back to these ideas is that they refuse to agree.

Qureshi makes me want to understand things from the inside rather than merely recognise their language, but complete understanding is rarely available before action is required. Feynman makes me value doubt, but excessive doubt can become paralysis. Hamming makes me want to choose important problems and protect them fiercely, while Pieper makes me suspicious of a life in which everything has become a problem to solve. Attention research argues for defending concentration, while the open door reminds me that too much protection has its own intellectual cost. Merton makes chance impossible to ignore, while preparation still changes what chance can become.

I used to think good principles should eventually converge into something coherent. I am less convinced now.

Perhaps coherence is sometimes overrated. A perfectly consistent philosophy can become impermeable to experience. It explains too much too easily. The more useful position may be to hold several truths close enough that they disturb one another.

Most virtues bend quietly into their opposites. Persistence becomes attachment to a bad model. Rigour becomes fear of committing. Focus becomes blindness. Openness becomes noise. Preparation becomes postponement. Efficiency becomes impoverishment. Ambition begins by enlarging a life and can end by narrowing it.

There is no rule that tells us exactly where the turn occurs. Context matters too much, and people are exceptionally talented at renaming their preferred failure mode as a virtue. The hesitant person can call hesitation rigour. The compulsively busy person can call motion ambition. The isolated person can call isolation focus.

Perhaps this is why I keep these ideas rather than trying to resolve them. Not because one of them is finally correct, but because each becomes useful when another has started to feel too comfortable.

I suppose that is what remains in all those scattered notes: not a system, and not quite advice. More like a set of interruptions, preserved for the moment when one explanation begins to look too complete.
