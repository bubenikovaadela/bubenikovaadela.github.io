---
title: "Elsewhere."
date: 2026-08-22 14:58:00 +0100
categories: [essays]
---

The longer I remain away, the less persuaded I am that home is a place at all. Increasingly, I understand it as a condition of consciousness, a kind of way of inhabiting the world without needing to possess a fragment of it and call that fragment mine. It is remarkable how quickly the apparent permanence of things begins to dissolve once one leaves them behind. Language, streets, familiar faces, the rhythm of a day, the tacit geography of what is near and what is distant, what is possible and what belongs to fantasy, all of it reveals itself as contingent. One crosses a border, changes a city, learns another cadence of life, and suddenly discovers that much of what had seemed like reality itself was only one local arrangement of reality, one interpretation among many.

Perhaps this is why I am so drawn to being elsewhere. Not because of the novelty of foreign places, nor because movement is inherently romantic, but because of what happens to the self when it is removed from the landscape that has learned to recognise it. In a foreign city, I have no history in the eyes of others. No one knows who I was, what I was once afraid of, what was expected of me, what I have failed at, what I have outgrown, or which versions of myself I have already abandoned. This does not make me another person in any superficial sense. Rather, it briefly releases me from the tyranny of my own biography. There is something almost sacred in that freedom, the possibility of standing at a slight distance from oneself and asking, with unusual clarity, which parts of one’s life are truly chosen and which have merely been repeated for so long that they began to resemble necessity.

Milan Kundera wrote of exile as a radical form of estrangement, a condition in which one becomes suspended between worlds. Yet what fascinates me is that this estrangement can also produce a peculiar intimacy with the world itself. To lose the unquestioned certainty of one home may be, paradoxically, to discover the vastness of belonging. Once a person has crossed enough borders, it becomes difficult to believe in them with the same seriousness. On maps they are precise, almost absolute, in lived experience they are strangely porous. A place that once seemed impossibly remote is suddenly composed of ordinary rain, stone pavements, tired faces on a morning bus, the smell of coffee through an open door. The distant becomes tangible. The symbolic becomes mundane. And in that transformation, something in us shifts as well.

I increasingly suspect that one of the great illusions of growing up is the belief that the world has already been apportioned. That there are places meant for us and places meant for other people, rooms we are entitled to enter and others before which we are expected to remain respectfully outside, lives that are considered realistic and lives that belong to a more audacious species of human being. Living abroad has slowly begun to dismantle that illusion. The more I see, the less convincing distance becomes as an argument. Some things are difficult, some profoundly improbable, some perhaps beyond reach entirely, but this is not the same as impossibility. Between “it cannot be done” and “I do not yet know how to do it” lies an enormous and exhilarating territory. I think I would like to spend my life there.

Much of this change comes from the people I encounter. I am fascinated by those who do not mistake a boundary for a conclusion. For them, an obstacle is not necessarily an injunction to stop; it is something to be examined. If there is a wall, they want to know how high it is, what it is made of, whether it can be climbed, circumvented, dismantled, or simply endured long enough to reach the other side. There is a rare kind of intellectual freedom in such people. It is not optimism, and certainly not naïveté. Often they are the ones who understand most clearly how unforgiving reality can be. What distinguishes them is that they do not confuse difficulty with prohibition.

Being among such people alters one’s internal measure of the world. I have begun to realise that many of the ceilings I once perceived above me were never structural features of reality, they belonged to the architecture of my own imagination. And when one of those ceilings disappears, the space above is almost vertiginous. This is perhaps what I mean when I say that even the sky does not feel like a limit. Not as a slogan, not as some sentimental declaration that everything is possible, but in a quieter and more disorienting sense: the world ceases to appear closed. It becomes unfinished, permeable, almost inexhaustible. Something into which one can continue moving.

What I love most about living elsewhere is precisely this conjunction of solitude and expansion. One is alone because familiar coordinates disappear, yet at the same time one seems to become larger because one is no longer defined by a single place. Solitude in a foreign city has a different texture from solitude at home. It is not necessarily emptiness; often it feels more like space. There is a peculiar silence in it, and within that silence things become audible that habit usually suppresses. Walking alone through a city that does not yet know me, I sometimes have the impression that there is nothing mediating between myself and the world. No role, no expectation, no accumulated history, no one else’s memory of who I am supposed to be. There is only consciousness and whatever lies before it. Strangely, these are often the moments in which I feel least alien to myself.

Perhaps this is why I do not feel that I become less myself abroad, but more so. Or, more precisely, I move closer to something in myself that is harder to reach at home. Every new place seems to remove something. A certainty, a fear, a small inherited conviction about what a life should look like. At the same time, it leaves something behind in me, such as another way of seeing, another possibility, another person whose presence remains long after the city itself has receded into memory. Eventually, the idea of a fixed self begins to seem questionable. Perhaps there is no singular, completed person travelling intact from one place to another. Perhaps we are accumulations of landscapes, languages, conversations, losses, ambitions, and all the selves that different places have permitted us to become.

This may also explain why the idea of a life that settles permanently into one geography feels somehow too final to me. Not because I want to flee. Flight presupposes a fixed point from which one is escaping. My feeling is almost the reverse, because I want to continue arriving. I want to arrive in unfamiliar cities, unfamiliar hospitals, unfamiliar intellectual worlds, among people whose way of thinking disturbs the comfort of my own. I want to retain a degree of rootlessness, not as an inability to belong, but as a refusal to allow belonging to become enclosure.

I often wonder whether we truly require one place to call home. Perhaps we do. Perhaps some part of us always longs for a single landscape to which everything can be referred back. But I am increasingly drawn to another possibility that home may not be something we stand upon, but something we carry as a manner of being. Not an address, but a capacity for relation;,not a point of origin, but an openness to the world. In that sense, any place, and whatever may come after it do not compete for the title of home. It just becomes a different chamber of the same existence.

And perhaps this is what I love most. The gradual discovery that the world is not something I must observe from one fixed position. It is something I can enter, cross, inhabit, lose myself in, and return from changed. That “elsewhere” need not mean distance. That “foreign” need not mean alien. That leaving is not necessarily a form of loss, and returning is not the only way of coming home.

Perhaps I will spend my life slightly elsewhere. Between places, between certainties, between versions of myself.

And the more I think about it, the more that seems not like exile, but like freedom.
