---
title: "The Risk of Stillness"
date: 2026-07-26
categories: [essays]
---

Human beings fear change because change promises nothing. It opens a door and refuses to show what lies beyond it. It asks us to abandon an order we understand without offering any assurance that the next one will be kinder. Perhaps it will not. Perhaps beyond the door there is no freedom, only another form of solitude.

Remaining, therefore, appears reasonable. We know the walls among which we live. We know their cracks, the hours at which the light crosses them, the silence that follows each familiar disappointment. We know what morning will demand of us and can almost predict what evening will leave behind. Such a life may be narrow, but it is legible. Its misery has acquired a grammar.

Change has always seemed to me like a storm, while stillness resembles the slow disappearance of air from a crowded room. The storm may wound us, but it does not allow us to doubt that we are alive. Rain runs across the face. Thunder enters the body. The heart reveals itself through its violence. For a moment, existence ceases to be an idea and becomes breath, skin, pulse.

Stillness is more discreet. It destroys nothing at once. It removes a small part of us each day, so quietly that no single loss seems worthy of resistance. Then one morning life remains where it has always been, intact in its furniture and obligations, but the person who was meant to live it is no longer entirely present.

This is the danger. Stillness does not strike like lightning. It persuades. It teaches a person that there is no reason to live differently from the way one has already learned to endure.

Not yet, it says. The moment is not right. One must wait. Leaving would be irresponsible.

Irresponsible toward what? Toward whom? Why?

Fear eventually learns the vocabulary of reason. That is when it becomes most convincing. We trust it precisely because it no longer sounds afraid.

But when does caution become escape? When does patience cease to be a virtue and become a slow consent to one's own disappearance?

Time does not wait for courage. It continues with the same indifference whether we are ready or not. I have never felt prepared for anything that truly altered my life. Perhaps preparation is only a story we tell ourselves when we wish to postpone the irreversible.

The decisions we do not make become, in time, as final as those we do. Inaction is also an act. Its cruelty lies in leaving behind no decisive moment, no visible wound, no hour to which we can later point and say: this is where it began.

A person does not fear movement alone. What one fears is that, once removed from familiar circumstances, one may cease to be the person one believed oneself to be.

How much of identity is merely an agreement between the self and its surroundings?

Many qualities exist only under conditions that have never tested them. Calm may be nothing more than a life without resistance. Courage may be a life that has not yet encountered danger. Loyalty may sometimes be only the inability to leave.

Who are we when the circumstances that confirmed us are taken away?

Does something essential remain, or do we discover that identity was a role performed so faithfully that we mistook it for a soul?

Change is merciless because it exposes these distinctions. It removes the old evidence by which a person recognized himself and leaves him, for a time, without an answer. The former self has lost its authority; the new one has not yet appeared. One stands between two lives, belonging fully to neither.

The sea of Odysseus may be understood as this interval. On land, a person possesses a name, a position, a history he can recite as proof of himself. At sea, there is only a body exposed to wind, exhaustion, hunger, and the indifference of the world. The waves do not care who he was on shore. They are unmoved by his plans, his virtues, or the importance he assigned to his return.

The sea owes him nothing.

This indifference is terrifying, but it also contains a severe kind of freedom. The world is not required to correspond to our hopes, and yet it remains possible to live within it. It need not send a sign that we have chosen correctly. It need not reward courage. It need not transform suffering into meaning.

How, then, are we to act in a world that remains silent?

How can one recognize the right road when no answer comes?

Is it possible to live without continually asking existence to certify our decisions?

Perhaps freedom begins there. Not when uncertainty disappears, but when we cease to demand certainty as the price of action. A person may then move without the consent of the world. Not because he knows where the road leads, but because he can no longer remain where he is without lying.

The absurd is not only that the world refuses to answer our questions. It is also that we continue to ask them. We want to know that the journey leads somewhere, that pain conceals a purpose, that courage will be repaid, and that whatever costs us dearly must therefore be right.

But what if no reward comes?

What if suffering contains no hidden instruction?

What if one leaves, loses everything familiar, and discovers nothing beyond it but another uncertainty?

A journey need not lead to a better life. It may lead only away from a life that could no longer be lived honestly. Sometimes that must be enough.

Fear does not vanish at such a moment. It is not defeated by revelation or by confidence in future happiness. One simply stops obeying it as though it were a commandment. One knows that failure is possible. Regret is possible. Loneliness is possible. The world will remain equally indifferent whether one departs or stays.

The decision is not made because meaning has been found. It is made because one refuses to continue living against what one has already understood.

It is a quiet revolt against collaboration with one's own disappearance.

How much of oneself can be sacrificed to peace before there is nothing left to protect?

And is a life still ours when it has been entirely arranged around the fear of losing it?

Growth rarely resembles ascent. More often, it resembles the loss of an old protection. A shell that once held a person together becomes too narrow. Breaking it is painful, but remaining inside requires life itself to bend, diminish, and finally take the shape of its confinement.

Comfort protects our established form because nothing new is allowed to question it. Discomfort removes this innocence. It reveals which convictions can withstand reality and which were merely repeated until repetition began to resemble truth.

Pain, however, guarantees nothing. It does not make a person wiser, nobler, or more profound. Sometimes it merely exhausts. Sometimes it produces bitterness or cruelty. Suffering is not evidence that a path is right. At most, it has the power to strip away certain illusions, and sometimes it fails even at that.

Must one suffer in order to grow? Certainly not.

But can one grow while refusing everything that might disturb the person one has already become? Can one know one's limits without approaching them? Can one know oneself while living only under conditions designed to keep the self unchanged?

There is no need to glorify the journey. It is neither pure nor just. It may change a person in ways he never desired. It may take more than it gives. It may lead to knowledge without consolation.

But stillness is not innocent either.

It changes us without spectacle, without catastrophe, without any event we can blame. Departure demands its price at once. Remaining collects it in fragments: one possibility today, another a year from now, another when we no longer remember that it was ever ours.

In the end, a person may discover that he spent his entire life protecting himself from risk, and that this protection deprived him of everything that had been worth protecting.

We do not stand between safety and danger. No such merciful choice is offered to us. We stand between two uncertainties: a world into which we may enter and lose ourselves, and a world in which we may remain until we are lost to ourselves.

Perhaps there is no correct path. Perhaps there is only a moment when it becomes impossible to continue pretending that stillness is neutral.

What is worse?

To lose, along the way, the person we once were—or never to meet the person we might have become?
