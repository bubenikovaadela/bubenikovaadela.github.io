Over the past month, I have noticed a growing fascination with the underground in myself, partly because everyone around me seems to be reading about it at the moment, and partly because I went mushroom picking yesterday.

The forests of Surrey have this peculiar feature called holloways, old sunken roads along which, for centuries, people travelled on foot, animals were driven, and carts moved between villages, pastures and markets. They formed gradually, as feet, hooves and wheels disturbed the surface, water carried away the loosened material, and the road slowly sank deeper into the ground. Over hundreds of years, some of them were cut several metres below the surrounding terrain. They give you the strange privilege of looking beneath the horizon of the soil you normally only walk across, so that suddenly entire root systems rise to the level of your eyes, which is quite extraordinary.

Root systems in general are endlessly fascinating things. Mechanically, they form a complex physical network that has to transfer the forces generated by gravity and wind into the surrounding soil, creating enough stability and balance to keep the tree upright while still allowing it a certain degree of movement. That stability depends not only on the wood itself, but on the geometry of the roots, their depth, orientation, flexural and torsional stiffness, and on the density, hydration, cohesion and compaction of the soil around them.

At the same time, a root system is a historical record of growth, local conditions, functional demands and continual remodelling. Its geometry is plastic. It is altered by the availability of water and nutrients, by oxygen, the mechanical impedance of the soil, rocks, compaction, drought, flooding and mechanical loading. To some extent, a root carries within its form a record of the environment through which it has grown during its lifetime, which is another way of saying that its shape is, in part, the history of the forces that have acted upon it.

Proximally, closer to the trunk, the larger roots are concerned mainly with mechanical support, transport and storage. The further distally you move away from the trunk, the more the system dissolves into finer networks. The finest absorptive roots are functionally rather similar to leaves. They have a high surface-area-to-volume ratio, take up water and mineral nutrients, and often interact extensively with mycorrhizal fungi. Mycorrhiza is another major category of my current fascination, but that is for another time. (Entangled Life by Merlin Sheldrake is brilliant.)

One of the things I find most fascinating about all of this is that a tree is really only an individual up to a certain point. It has its own trunk, its own genetic identity and its own history of growth, but without soil, water, fungi, bacteria, sunlight and other organisms, it cannot exist as a tree. It is a recognisable organism, and yet its existence depends upon a network of relationships extending far beyond the boundaries of its own cells. Paradoxically, what is least visible has often been the most essential to its growth all along, and continues to shape what it will become.

By analogy, a human being is not any less of an individual simply because they are the result of relational interactions. Some definition of what I call myself arises within a community, a language, a culture, a family, institutions, opportunities, landscape, history and other people. Just as we rarely notice the roots we are constantly walking over because they are hidden beneath a few centimetres of soil and grass, we also fail to see most of the things that have shaped the person standing in front of us.

Are you defined more by the colour of your skin, or by the conditions in which you grew up? By the language you speak, or by the people who taught you to think through that language? By religion? By family?

Of course, all of these things matter. They are not irrelevant, they shape experience, opportunity and the way the surrounding world responds to you. But they are still only some of the variables in a much more complicated equation.

Whether, for our growth, we were given dry sand, little water and stones, or a rich, damp mountain grove watered by rain every week, is not a property of us alone, of us as the tree. It is a condition of our growth. And it would be slightly absurd to place two mature trees beside one another, measure their height, and claim that we had measured their capacity to grow without ever looking at the soil in which each of them had grown. Equally absurd is the idea that we all begin from the same starting line.

Perhaps the more important question, then, is how we approach humanity as a concept. The idea that beneath all those categories, we are still one species, astonishingly plastic and deeply dependent on the conditions around us. That we are dynamic in much the same way trees are, and that sometimes we have to begin below before we can grow upwards at all.

That perhaps it matters rather less whether the leaves are red, green or yellow. Autumn is beautiful precisely because of its variability, its dynamism, its patience and its transience. The leaves will fall eventually anyway. Unless you are a conifer, you pedant, but even your needles dry out a little and are not exactly at peak freshness in winter.

And by spring, you are slightly different again.

Beneath us lies an entire universe of complex interactions between soil, rock, water, roots, fungi and microorganisms. We walk over it every day and barely register its existence, because we are occupied with whatever is happening above the surface, with whatever our visual cortex allows us to see and process.

I love roots. I am fascinated by their mechanics, their plasticity, their hidden beauty, and by the way the boundaries of a single organism suddenly stop seeming quite so self-evident once you look underground.
