description: "On memory as interaction: what remains in bodies, places, objects, and systems after the event itself has gone."
---

Perhaps memory is not a faculty of consciousness but a consequence of interaction.

A stone taken from a river appears silent, yet its form is historical. Its surface is the remainder of vanished forces. The river's pressure, friction, impact, current. None of them is present any longer, and still the stone would not be what it is without them. The river has not been preserved as an image. It has been translated into structure.

Perhaps this is the more fundamental meaning of memory.

Not the return of what happened, but the impossibility of returning entirely to what existed before it happened.

Physics is full of such asymmetries. At the quantum level, a system is not always best imagined as possessing one definite classical history before interaction. It may instead be described by a superposition of possibilities, amplitudes distributed over several potential outcomes. Interaction with the environment does not merely reveal a world that was already waiting in finished form. Through decoherence, possibilities become effectively separated, and the world acquires a history.

What is striking is not merely that one outcome occurs. It is that the alternatives cease to remain available to us in the same way. Perhaps every history is built from this disappearance of alternatives.

Before the interaction, several futures can still belong to the description of the system. Afterwards there is a record, somewhere in the environment, in emitted radiation, in altered matter, in correlations between one system and another. The event has dispersed itself into the world. To undo it would not mean moving one particle backwards. It would require reconstructing an immense web of correlations with impossible precision.

In this sense, the universe does not remember because it possesses an archive. It remembers because events leave consequences in other things. A system touches another system, and neither is quite independent afterward.

There is something almost unsettling in this. We ordinarily imagine existence as a succession of isolated moments, the present replacing the past as a new image replaces the previous frame of a film. But perhaps the present is never clean enough for such replacement. Every interaction leaves information distributed somewhere, often beyond recovery but not necessarily beyond consequence.

The past does not remain behind us as it becomes entangled with what follows.

Not necessarily in the strict quantum-mechanical sense in which two particles share a nonseparable state, but in a broader and perhaps more philosophically disturbing one, that once things have interacted, their later states cannot always be understood as though the encounter had never occurred.

A stone is the river after the water has gone.

A person may be something similar.

We speak as though there were first a self and then the experiences that happened to it. Yet perhaps no such untouched self ever persists. Each encounter changes the conditions under which the next encounter will be received. What has happened modifies not only memory but possibility.

This is why the past can survive without being remembered.

A forgotten event may no longer return as an image, yet it can remain as probability, a hesitation, an attraction, a fear whose origin has disappeared, a path chosen so often that eventually it begins to resemble character.

The river deepens the channel through which it flows, the altered channel then biases the direction of the next current.

At some point cause becomes constraint. Constraint becomes repetition. Repetition becomes identity. One might then ask whether identity itself is merely a history whose alternatives have decohered.

There were perhaps many possible selves, not mystical doubles waiting in parallel worlds, but unrealized trajectories, like decisions not made, people not met, cities not entered, convictions never acquired. With every event, the field narrows. One version gains actuality while countless others retreat into counterfactual existence.

We call this becoming, but becoming is also a form of loss. Every definite state contains the disappearance of possibilities that once surrounded it. Perhaps this is why returning is impossible. One can reconstruct the room, the street, even the person. But one cannot reconstruct the state of the observer before the first encounter. The later self now contains information the earlier one did not possess. It has been altered by what followed.

To return truly would require more than restoring the coordinates. One would have to restore superposition. One would have to make the unrealized possibilities available again, remove knowledge without preserving the knowledge that it had been removed, erase every correlation created by what happened afterwards.

The world does not permit such innocence easily.

Perhaps nostalgia is the emotional form of this impossibility. We imagine we miss a place because a place can still be pointed to. But what we miss may be the wider field of possibilities that existed there before one of them became real.

A room before the sentence was spoken, a person before we knew what they would become to us, a journey before we knew how it would end. The longing is not for space. It is for an earlier distribution of possibility.

And perhaps this is the deepest memory carried by things, not simply that something happened, but that by happening it changed what could happen next.

The river no longer touches the stone, the event no longer exists, the alternatives are gone, and yet the present bears their absence.

Perhaps that is what time ultimately is, not the movement of moments past us, but the continuous conversion of possibility into history.

And memory is the shape left behind.
