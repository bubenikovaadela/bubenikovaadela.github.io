When I travel, I do feel changes in my own perception of many things simply because the experiences I encounter shape essentially everything I perceive afterwards, and that cannot be taken back. If we make the definition broader, memory is perhaps not merely a faculty of consciousness. Rather, it is a consequence of interaction with all our previous experiences.

To illustrate the concept, a stone taken from a river may seem static, yet its form carries the history of what has shaped it. Its surface is the remainder of vanished forces — the river’s pressure, friction, impact, current. All the water molecules, all the other stones, wood, and whatever else has run over it. All of these things are long gone, and still the stone would not be what it is without them. The river has written itself into its structure, into a stone that cannot be entirely separated from the river anymore.

This is probably a more fundamental meaning of memory. Not the return of what happened, but the impossibility of returning entirely to what existed before it happened.

Physics is full of such asymmetries. At the quantum level, a system cannot always be thought of as having followed a single, definite classical history before it interacts with its environment. Instead, it may be described by a superposition of possibilities, with amplitudes distributed over several potential outcomes. When a quantum system — which can be something as ordinary as a molecule — interacts with its surroundings, the different possibilities it contains no longer remain perfectly coherent with one another. Their ability to interfere is progressively lost, a process known as decoherence. What was once a set of overlapping possibilities then begins to behave more like a set of distinct alternatives, with interactions leaving physical traces of what has happened.

What is striking is not simply that one outcome occurs, but that the alternatives no longer remain accessible in the same way. A history begins to take shape precisely because some possibilities are realised while others recede from what can still happen. You choose the cheese roll instead of the spinach roll, and that essentially shapes everything around it — the present and the future.

Before the interaction, several futures can still belong to the description of the system. Afterwards, there is a record somewhere in the environment, in emitted radiation, in altered matter, in correlations between one system and another, and the event — in our case, eating the roll — has dispersed itself into the world. To reverse it would mean restoring the exact state of the particle, its surroundings, and every interaction through which information about that event had already spread. You cannot undo eating your roll. Or perhaps you can, but you would need to change its basic definition, shape, and consistency, thus generating something completely different.

In this sense, the universe does not have an archive, iCloud, Drive, or anything of that sort in which it remembers all these things. It remembers everything because the events that happen leave consequences in other things. A system touches another system, and neither is quite independent afterwards.

We as humans ordinarily imagine existence as a succession of isolated moments. We tend to think chronologically, with the present state replacing the past state as a new image replaces the previous frame of a film — another doom scroll, another picture. But perhaps the present is never clean enough for such replacement. Every interaction leaves information distributed somewhere, often beyond recovery — back to our roll thing — but not necessarily beyond consequence. You did, after all, resolve your roll craving.

The past does not remain behind us. It becomes entangled with what follows.

Not necessarily in the strict quantum-mechanical sense in which two particles share a nonseparable state, but in a broader, and perhaps more philosophically disturbing, sense. Once things have interacted, their later states cannot always be understood individually as though the encounter had never occurred. We often speak as though there were first a self and then the experiences that happened to it. However, when you think about it, no such untouched self ever persists. Each encounter changes the conditions under which the next encounter will be received, which is also why what has happened modifies not only memory but all future possibilities. Your decision now changes everything that comes after it. This is why the past can survive without being remembered. It is imprinted in everything inside and around you.

A forgotten event may no longer return as an image, yet it can remain as a probability, a hesitation, an attraction, a fear whose origin has disappeared, a path chosen so often that eventually it begins to resemble character, the identification of yourself. Similarly, the river deepens the channel through which it flows, and the altered channel, as a result of all that time and all those physical forces, then biases the direction of the next current.

At some point, cause becomes constraint, constraint becomes repetition, repetition becomes identity. We might then ask whether identity itself is merely a history whose alternatives have decohered.

I have come to realise — or let’s say, this is my own understanding — that there are many possible selves. Not mystical doubles waiting in parallel worlds, but unrealised trajectories: decisions not made, people not met, cities not entered, convictions never acquired. With each event, the spectrum of available possibilities narrows. One possibility becomes part of what actually happened, while the others remain only as paths that we might have chosen but never actually did.

We call this becoming, but becoming is also a form of loss. Every definite state contains the disappearance of possibilities that once surrounded it, and perhaps this is also why returning is impossible. I can vividly reconstruct the room, the street, even the person, but I simply cannot reconstruct the state of myself as an observer before that first encounter. My later self now contains information that my earlier self simply did not possess because it has been altered by what followed.

To truly return would require more than restoring the coordinates. Returning to the cheese roll example, we would somehow have to restore not only its position, but the full quantum state that existed before the alternatives became effectively separated. Someone could argue, using my other stone example, that you would not actually see the difference between the state in which you put the stone into the river and its state after one year. Macroscopically? Very unlikely. Microscopically? At the quantum level? Hell yeah, you would see a difference. It is also a matter of scale and the amount of change.

In order to truly return, we would have to make the unrealised possibilities available again, remove knowledge without preserving the knowledge that it had been removed, and erase every correlation created by what happened afterwards. And that’s hard stuff.

From an emotional perspective, nostalgia is a similar form of impossibility. We imagine that we miss a place because a place can still be pointed to. But what we are more likely to miss may be the wider field of possibilities that existed there before one of them became real.

A room before the sentence was spoken, a person before we knew what they would become to us, a journey before we knew how it would end. The longing is not for the space itself. It is for an earlier distribution of possibility. And maybe this is the deepest memory carried by things: not simply that something happened, but that by happening, it changed what could happen next.

The river no longer touches the stone, the event no longer exists, the alternatives are gone, and yet the present bears their absence.

Perhaps that is what time ultimately is — not the movement of moments past us, but the continuous conversion of possibility into history.

And memory is the shape left behind.
