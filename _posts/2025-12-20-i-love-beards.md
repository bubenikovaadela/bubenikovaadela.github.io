title: "I love beards."
date: 2025-12-20 08:00:00 +0100

I love beards.

An absurd opening, really—because it betrays me at once, exposes the very thing I otherwise take such care to deny. That I am pure interpretation. That I am some elevated spectator of the world, the sort who cannot be rattled. And yet all it takes is a scatter of hairs on a man’s face and my entire philosophical contraption collapses back into the flat, faithful monotony of the everyday. Perhaps that is for the best. Perhaps it is the only honesty I still know how to afford.

I am sitting in a café, performing industriousness. A book lies open before me at a page I worry between finger and thumb. A paper mask—respectable, because society has agreed that books count as virtue. The book informs the room that I am occupied. It also grants me what I actually want: permission to be silent; to look; to be present; and, at the same time, to appear as though I am not.

A random assortment of people gathered in one place. A heterogeneity that resembles accident but is, in fact, only the law of large numbers wearing a casual coat. Trajectories that would ordinarily miss one another—briefly crossing, aligning, sharing the same light and the same low hiss of the room—before separating again as though nothing had happened, and forgetting one another with impressive efficiency. Each person carries their own small, private absolute. Each of us keeps a centre of the world inside our head and behaves, quite sensibly, as though the rest revolves around it. After all, what we truly have access to is mostly our own data—data we share with others only partially, and always according to our own peculiar grammar.

And of course I begin to assign meaning to these trajectories. The brain has a horror of emptiness. It is a machine for models. Give it a handful of points and it will insist on a curve. From the way two hands are held it will infer temperament. From a gaze fixed on nothing it will conjure a past. From the faintest tremor of unease it will manufacture guilt, or at least a story that makes the unease bearable—something that explains it so the mind does not have to simply endure it. It invents lives because chance is cold and narrative is warm. And because, when my mind retells the world to itself, I am permitted—briefly—to feel that I understand it. A small attempt at mastery over the chaos of living.

I know I am misleading myself. The trouble is that self-deception has an uncanny talent for dressing as sensitivity. And sensitivity is remarkably good at selling itself to itself. I tell myself I see more than other people do, when the truth is only that I have imagined more. Sometimes I catch myself entertaining the mortifying thought that there ought to be an ICD code for it somewhere in my medical notes—an official designation for the compulsive interpretation of strangers’ faces. And then I understand why the idea makes me so cross. Because to name a thing is to tame it. To cut it down to fit a category; to file it; to close it. And certain things fall apart at precisely that moment, because their substance is movement: they are dynamic, chaotic, alive—misty, unfinished.

That is why it is easy to describe a pendulum at school, and nearly impossible to describe it truthfully. We reduce it to an image we can bear. We ignore the surrounding particulars—friction, currents of air, minute shifts in the suspension, vibrations from the street—and in doing so we create a system that is, in reality, ferociously complex. Consciously and unconsciously we cannot tolerate the sensation of not knowing, of being outmatched by the world’s complexity. So I may either rage at reality for refusing to behave according to my model, or accept that a model is only a way of looking. In that sense, my stories about strangers are models too: elegant, useful, and very far from the whole truth.

It is curious how effortlessly we perceive ourselves above all else. How readily a person makes their own self into a coordinate system. Sitting among people, but in fact sitting inside a private monologue—rehearsing the same text again and again, only rearranged, re-intoned, burdened with a different emphasis of blame. The world becomes scenery. An economy of attention, certainly—but one that comes at a price. The price is that reality grows poorer. And yet reality is richest precisely where it refuses to be translated into a single definition.

A smile can be greeting or defence. Silence can be peace or punishment. “Fine” can mean agreement, conclusion, or a quiet plea that no one open any further doors. People speak in how close they stand; in how quickly they look away; in how much space they permit themselves—or how they withdraw from it, as though existence itself were an offence. And we often choose the simpler reading because it is comfortable—because it shields us from the fact that the world holds several meanings at once, and none of them is ever wholly secure. Meaning is not certainty; it is only greater or lesser likelihood.

Sometimes, though, something shifts. Not by argument. Not by wisdom. Merely by some detail that, at first pass, appears negligible. Something small catches in the mind and suddenly it illuminates: the people around you are not props. They are wholes—only opened at a random page. A few molecules in the current of a river that will flow on perfectly well without their opinion. In that moment it can feel unpleasant, because it deprives people of their sense of importance. And yet it can also feel like relief, because it removes the obligation to be perpetually, oppressively serious.

And in the end, I always return to earth. The movement that ruins my pose and rescues me from my own solemnity. A bearded man passes by, and all these thoughts—about perspective, about masks, about truth as a construction of angles—contract into a single quiet, bodily sentence. No evidence. No system. No performance required. And I realise something that is, in a way, embarrassingly ordinary: the observer cannot be separated from the observed. Even my attempts to quantify the world are shaped by the fact that I am only one particular person, with my senses, my preferences, my weaknesses. And that is not an error. It is simply the condition of the experiment.

Beards are godlike.
