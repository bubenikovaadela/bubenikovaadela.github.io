Balance.  
Medicine can’t resist giving it a proper name, so it calls it *homeostasis*. The body, in that story, is a thermostat: nudge it away from center and it dutifully slides back, again and again. That return is treated as the goal, the mark of health, the thing we should want.

But physics has a colder word for that: **equilibrium**. The end of the action. The perfectly flattened finale. It looks like an ideal—until you remember what it means for anything alive. Because for a living creature, perfect balance is not serenity.

It’s disaster.

When nothing flows, you already know what that means.

So no: homeostasis cannot be equilibrium.  
Homeostasis is not “balance.”  
Homeostasis is **imbalance, continuously held in place**.

Physiology textbooks lie.

Life isn’t defined by what stays still. Life is defined by what won’t. By what shifts, sways, corrects, overshoots, returns—not once, but constantly, and on several clocks at the same time. Like an orchestra: double basses and timpani hauling the slow undercurrent forward while, above them, violins and flutes stitch quick figures and tremolos into the air, the fine-grained movement that gives the whole thing texture. Without that living shimmer, you don’t have music—you have a tone.

Take heart rate variability: the simple fact that the gaps between beats are not identical. Those tiny mismatches carry the fingerprints of the autonomic nervous system, the rise and fall of breath, stress, recovery, vigilance, fatigue—everything a body is doing without consulting you.

And the crucial point is this: the irregularity is not grime on the measurement.  
It *is* the measurement.

A physicist would say: make it perfectly periodic and you haven’t found life—you’ve built a metronome. You’ve forced an oscillator from the outside, like a pendulum sealed behind glass in a laboratory cabinet. Living oscillators don’t behave like that. They wrestle with themselves. They carry phase noise. They wander. They fluctuate in ways you can’t polish out, no matter how obsessively you try.

That restlessness isn’t a mistake.  
It’s anatomy.

Fractality in heart rate is, at its core, a kind of long memory. Long-range correlations: what happened far back still leans—ever so slightly—on what happens now. As if the heart were not a clock, but a narrator: remembering previous sentences, shaping the next one with echoes of what came before. Nonlinearity. Feedback loops. State variables that don’t snap back along the shortest path, but drag a little hysteresis behind them like a coat hem in the rain.

And then there is noise—entering not as sabotage, but as bedrock. The quantum undertone here is almost insolent. Even if you isolate everything, perfectly, there will still be grain. A hard limit. A spread you cannot smooth away. Not an error bar you can shrink to zero, but a property of the world.

And then comes the truly gorgeous thought: maybe it isn’t just *one* fractal—one tidy “degree of irregularity” you can pin down with a single number. Maybe it’s **multifractal**: a whole spectrum of roughness, a wide probability landscape, many distinct ways of being uneven and still being most fully yourself.

Health, then, is not a point.  
It’s a region.

And when the heart begins to fail, that region contracts. The distribution narrows. Options vanish. Degrees of freedom quietly disappear. Like music collapsing into a single note that no longer goes anywhere.

The bronchial tree tells a similar story. At first it looks comforting—beautifully textbook, almost architectural. From a distance it resembles clean geometry, the branching close to exponential, disciplined, symmetrical… for a while.

Then, past a certain depth, it starts to slip. The pattern frays. Branches split at odd angles. One runs long, another short. The neatness dissolves into something closer to improvisation.

Why?

Not because nature “messed up,” but because *we* smuggled in symmetry as an expectation and mistook it for law. Symmetry is expensive. It costs energy. It costs control. It costs precision. Living tissue isn’t milled from metal; it grows, repairs itself, adapts while it’s being used.

A bronchial tree isn’t merely plumbing. It’s a conversation between structure and flow. Flow remodels the wall; the wall redirects the flow. And the “noise” you see isn’t just instrument error.

Fluctuations give a system spring. The ability to sway without freezing. To wobble rather than lock. Sometimes, to hop a threshold in the right direction. Deterministic laws sketch the terrain—but fluctuations often decide which path is actually taken across it.

That is why health may look less like perfect regularity and more like **rich, structured irregularity**.

Perfect regularity feels like an ideal only because it’s easy to describe. But what it often is, in practice, is the **loss of degrees of freedom**—the system nailed to one rhythm, unable to dodge, unable to adapt, unable to be surprised. A crude, naïve simplification of the world, built to flatter our models.

So let’s drop the fantasy.

Imbalance moves the world.

Here’s to a beautiful new year—  
and to plenty of healthy imbalance.
