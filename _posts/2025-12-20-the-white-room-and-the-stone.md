---
title: "The White Room and the Stone."
date: 2026-05-29 08:00:00 +0100
categories: [essays]
---

I love medicine because it gives me a stone to push, and without that stone the mind becomes nothing but a hill beneath an indifferent sun, a place where the same thought must be carried upward each morning only to fall again by evening. There is no revelation at the summit, no voice, no judgment, no mercy. There is only the weight, the ascent, and the knowledge that the descent has already begun.
Rest, for me, is not peace but the valley where I hear the stone returning.

People speak of calm as if it were a natural country, as if the mind, left alone, would become a quiet animal sleeping in the shade. Mine does not. In stillness it expands until it fills the room, and the old questions begin to breathe again with their useless patience. Why suffer, why desire, why continue, why wake into a body that is already losing its argument with time. They come not as thoughts, but as weather. They gather behind the eyes, press against the skull, and turn every silence into a room where something wounded is pacing.

There are hours when I cannot bear the private noise of being myself. Nothing dramatic happens. No great catastrophe arrives. The world continues with its ordinary cruelty, cups on tables, cars passing outside, messages unanswered, light moving across a wall. But inside, some tribunal convenes again. Memory enters with its documents. Shame takes the chair. Desire testifies badly. Every old failure becomes suddenly articulate, and I am asked to defend a life I never fully understood how to live.

This is why idleness frightens me more than labor. Work exhausts the body, but stillness interrogates the soul. In rest, I become too available to my own darkness, and the darkness is not poetic then, not noble, not deep in any useful sense. It is merely intimate. It knows where to place its hand. It knows which door in the mind opens onto the oldest room.
Medicine does not answer this suffering, and this is why I trust it.

It has the dignity not to console. It does not cover pain with meaning, nor does it tell the wounded man that the universe has leaned closer to hear him. Medicine does not soften the absurd. It switches on the light and asks where the bleeding is.
Under that light, suffering loses its theatrical fog and becomes precise. It becomes pressure, swelling, hemorrhage, silence in a pupil. It becomes a shadow on a scan, a vessel under the hand, a field that can be entered. The invisible wound receives a border. The metaphysical animal is given anatomy. This, perhaps, is the first mercy. Not hope, not explanation, but form.

I think my mind has always been hungry for form. Left alone, it becomes a sea at night, black, repetitive, full of movements that cannot be named. It invents storms because no coast interrupts it. Medicine gives it a coast. Neurosurgery gives it a cliff. The operating room gives it a narrow ledge on which every step matters, and there, for a while, the sea cannot speak.

The hospital before dawn is a country without tenderness, and still I return to it as one returns to the only country where one is understood. Its corridors are pale, its lamps sleepless, its machines already speaking in their cold little dialects. Somewhere, a monitor counts the life of a stranger with the patience of a clerk. Somewhere, a body moves through the blue hour beneath a sheet, carried not toward salvation but toward an attempt.

No one speaks loudly there, not because the place is holy, but because everyone knows what waits beneath the drape.

The operating room is not a sanctuary. It is a desert made white. There is no God in it, only attention, no forgiveness, only precision, no victory, only the next necessary gesture. Yet I enter it almost gratefully, because within its severity I am spared the luxury of myself. The self is loud in empty rooms, but in the OR it must lower its voice. It must stand aside. It must let the hand see.
The sound of suction, the cold order of steel, the monitor with its small tyranny, the blue drapes, the gloved hands, the silence before incision, all of it gathers the mind into a single point. I do not become happy there. Happiness would be too domestic a word for such light. I become exact, and exactness is sometimes the only mercy given to those who cannot rest.
A hand can be steadier than a soul.

This is one of the few truths I have learned.

In neurosurgery, the absurd becomes almost intimate. The skull opens, and inside there is no throne, no immortal flame, no secret monarch governing the self. There is tissue, pale and wet, obedient to blood, threatened by swelling, vulnerable to the smallest violence. And yet from this tissue a child once recognized his mother, a woman remembered the sea, a man felt shame, desire, fear, music, tenderness, and the stubborn wish not to die.

To stand before the brain is to stand before the humiliation and grandeur of man at once. All our metaphysics, all our prayers, all our private theatres of longing and guilt are gathered in a fragile substance that can be silenced by pressure, deprived by a vessel, erased by a swelling no larger than a fruit. The self, which feels infinite from within, is almost nothing from the outside. A little water, a little blood, a little electricity. A kingdom balanced on vessels thinner than thread.

The human being is ridiculous, but he is also unbearable. He is a little warmth organized against the dark, a flame persuaded for a while to live in flesh. He knows he will be defeated, and still he asks for one more morning.

Medicine is the art of that morning.

It does not defeat death. It delays it with poor tools and magnificent stubbornness. It pushes the stone upward for one more hour, knowing the stone will fall, knowing the hill is endless, knowing that every recovery is provisional and every healed body only a reprieve written in disappearing ink. But the gesture is made, and perhaps the gesture is all the meaning we are allowed.

That is enough.

I think I love medicine because I understand this kind of futility, the work that does not save in any final sense but refuses, the dignity of repetition, the washing of hands, the entering of the room, the opening of the body, the closing of the wound, the beginning again. The world offers no acquittal, so the hand must become its own rebellion.

In the OR, my unrest is not wasted. It is harnessed. Sadness becomes vigilance, fear becomes discipline, hunger for punishment becomes endurance, and the black animal in the skull is given a rope, a slope, a stone. Adrenaline is not joy but clarity under command. Exhaustion is not ruin but the mind emptied of useless mirrors. Calm is not peace but panic taught to stand upright.

There is a masochism in this, perhaps, but not the childish worship of pain. It is the need to be pressed against necessity until the self stops performing, until all decorative suffering burns away and only attention remains. Some people are saved by softness. Others are made coherent only by weight. I belong to the weight.

I do not love suffering. I love the instant when suffering is met, not explained, not justified, not redeemed, but met with a clamp, a drill, a stitch, a hand that continues after comfort has disappeared. There is a strange tenderness in being useful while broken. The wound inside me does not close, but it learns posture. It stands beside the table. It watches the field. It holds the light.

Perhaps this is all I have ever wanted from pain, not that it vanish, not that it be forgiven, but that it be converted into something that does not humiliate me. A pressure that becomes precision. A loneliness that becomes attention. A restlessness that becomes readiness. The mind that once circled itself like an animal in a cage now circles the wound, and for a while this is almost freedom.
Man is broken, yes, but he is not only broken. He is the creature who returns to the hill, who places his shoulder against the stone though he has already seen it fall, who scrubs his hands beneath a merciless light and says, without hope and without surrender, not yet. Not here. Not while there is still a pulse beneath the fingers.

When the operation ends, nothing final has been conquered. The gloves come off, the body is closed, the room begins to forget us, and outside the morning waits with its indifferent sky. Still, something has been postponed. Someone may wake. Someone may speak. Someone may return, damaged but present, to the difficult country of the living.

So I return too, not healed, not saved, not peaceful in any ordinary sense, but lucid enough to begin again.

I wash my hands.

I enter.

I listen to the machines begin their cold hymn.

And for a little while, under the white light, the stone is mine.
