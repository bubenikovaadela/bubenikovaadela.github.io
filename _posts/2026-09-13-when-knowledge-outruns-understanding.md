---
title: "When Knowledge Outruns Understanding."
date: 2026-09-13 08:00:00 +0100
categories: [essays]
---

There is something faintly comic about the current debate over AI and mathematics. A group of distinguished mathematicians[^1] warns that machines may soon begin solving important problems too quickly. Open questions which have sustained generations of ideas, methods and mathematicians may disappear before the community has had time to extract from them what really matters. Michael Eisen[^2] then takes almost the same argument and rewrites it in the language of cancer biology. AI begins curing cancers too quickly, producing too many cancer free patients and depriving the field of the fertile intellectual ground on which future generations might otherwise have worked.

Suddenly, something that sounded almost profound in mathematics becomes absurd and it is precisely why the parody works. It does not really refute the mathematicians. It exposes the uncomfortable possibility that science sometimes romanticises its own difficulty.

Years of work, failed experiments, abandoned proofs, blind alleys and ideas that take decades to mature genuinely produce something. They cultivate judgement, intuition, patience and taste. A difficult problem can shape a person long before that person comes close to solving it. But it does not follow that difficulty itself is valuable. If a problem can be solved in an hour rather than ten years, the ten years of frustration do not retrospectively become a virtue.

Eisen is particularly sharp on this point. Academic communities can easily confuse the purpose of a problem with the purpose of the community that has formed around it. An obstacle gradually becomes an ecosystem. Schools, conferences, grants, reputations and careers accumulate around its resistance to solution. Eventually it can become difficult to distinguish between defending the value of knowledge and defending the social world built around producing it.

In medicine, the distinction is impossible to ignore. A patient with glioblastoma does not exist to provide us with an intellectually fertile problem. If an AI system discovered an effective treatment tomorrow, nobody would seriously argue that we ought to slow it down because we had thereby lost twenty years of beautiful mechanistic biology.

But mathematics is not a patient.

This is where Eisen's analogy begins to break. In medicine, success has a value partly external to our understanding of it. A patient survives. A tumour regresses. A disease becomes preventable. We may still want to understand the mechanism, but the achievement does not cease to matter because the explanation remains incomplete.

Mathematics is different in a way that matters. A proof does not merely close a question. At its best, it changes the shape of the surrounding landscape. It shows why something must be true, what else follows from it, which assumptions were essential and which were accidental. The theorem is not simply the destination. Much of the mathematics is contained in the route by which one reaches it.

And here the mathematicians are right.

A machine could, in principle, produce thousands of formally correct proofs that other machines verify perfectly, while human mathematicians extract from them little beyond the knowledge that certain statements are true. That would unquestionably constitute progress of some kind. But it would force us to separate two ideas that until now we have rarely had to distinguish very carefully. The expansion of knowledge and the expansion of understanding.

Historically, we have allowed them to coincide.

To discover something was usually to understand enough of the surrounding structure to reach it. Scientific progress therefore also enlarged the space of human thought. New physics did not merely give us new predictions. It gave us new ways of imagining matter, time and causation. New biology did not simply produce interventions. It changed what we meant by inheritance, development and disease. Mathematics did not merely accumulate true statements. It developed new objects with which humans could think.

AI may reveal that this connection was contingent rather than necessary.

That possibility is more interesting than the question of whether AI will take work away from mathematicians. Professions change. Nor is the claim that students need unsolved problems ultimately sufficient. We cannot justify preserving ignorance because ignorance happens to be educationally useful.

The deeper possibility is that scientific capability may begin to advance faster than human comprehension.

This need not arrive dramatically. In biology, a model may identify a drug combination that works unusually well. The result replicates. The clinical effect is real. The mechanism remains obscure. We use it, as we should. Then another result appears, and another. Eventually we may find ourselves relying on systems that can intervene in biological processes with extraordinary precision while representing those processes in ways that no longer correspond neatly to the concepts in which human scientists explain them.

There is nothing obviously wrong with that. In fact, there is something slightly narcissistic about insisting that truth becomes valuable only once it has been rendered intelligible to us. The world has no obligation to respect the cognitive limits of our species. Human intuition evolved under very particular conditions. There is no reason to assume that every important structure in mathematics or biology should fit comfortably within it.

If AI gives us access to regularities that we can discover and use but never make fully intuitive, rejecting them simply because they exceed us would be a strange form of intellectual vanity.

But the opposite conclusion is too easy as well.

Understanding is not a decorative layer placed on top of a successful prediction. It is what allows us to know when an analogy breaks, when a model has moved outside its domain and when an apparently convincing result should make us suspicious rather than obedient. It gives us the ability to generalise rather than merely repeat. A system that tells us what works may be extraordinarily useful. A theory tells us something about why the next case may be different.

Without that distinction, scientific knowledge risks becoming something closer to infrastructure. Immensely powerful, indispensable and increasingly opaque to the people who depend on it.

We already tolerate this in much of modern life. Almost nobody needs to understand semiconductor physics to use a phone. But science has traditionally claimed something more ambitious for itself. Its purpose was not merely to make the world more manipulable. It was also to make the world more intelligible.

Perhaps this is the real tension exposed by these two texts.

The mathematicians are right that an open problem can be valuable before it is solved. Eisen is right that this does not give us a reason to keep it unsolved. One side sees what can be lost when the route disappears. The other sees the absurdity of protecting the route at the expense of reaching the destination.

But framing the disagreement as a choice between those two positions misses the more unsettling possibility.

The route and the destination may no longer belong to the same agent.

A machine may reach the result first. Humans may spend months or years reconstructing what it means, discovering which parts matter, connecting it to existing ideas and eventually turning it into something we would recognise as understanding. Sometimes we may succeed completely. Sometimes only partially. And sometimes the distance between capability and comprehension may remain.

Science might increasingly take place in that distance. This would not make machine produced results worthless, nor would it make human understanding obsolete. It would simply end a very old assumption. When the frontier of knowledge moves forward, the frontier of the human mind does not necessarily move with it.

That assumption has been so deeply built into scientific culture that we rarely notice it. We speak of what we know as though knowledge were automatically something available to human thought. But a civilisation can already know more than any individual within it understands. Its knowledge is distributed across people, instruments, institutions, databases and languages. AI may simply extend that distribution beyond humans.

The question is therefore not whether this counts as knowledge. That debate may ultimately be semantic.

The more interesting question is what kind of relationship we want to have with knowledge that outruns us.

We should not preserve unsolved problems simply because struggling with them has historically made us better scientists. Nor should we mistake an ever growing supply of correct answers for an ever deepening understanding of the world.

AI may force us to hold both thoughts at once. Removing intellectual obstacles can be genuine progress, yet something important is lost when the answer arrives without bringing understanding with it.

The question "Should AI solve open problems?" therefore already feels too small.

Whether we want it to or not, it increasingly will.

What matters is what happens after the answer arrives.
